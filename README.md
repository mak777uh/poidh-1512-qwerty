# The QWERTY slowdown myth — a "fact" born in a 1985 economics paper

**The claim being debunked:** *"The QWERTY keyboard was designed to slow typists down so the typebars
would not jam."* It is repeated as established fact — and it is not supported by the primary record.

---

## 1. Where the claim circulates (verified form, source by source)

**Asserted as fact:**
1. **ExplainTheOrdinary, 2 April 2026** — "The QWERTY Sabotage: Our Keyboards Were *Literally Designed*
   to Slow Us Down": *"It was arguably designed to slow you down"*; the article states that the layout was
   arranged because "mechanists wanted to alternate hand strokes to give the machine time to reset".
   https://explaintheordinary.com/blog/object-origins/the-qwerty-sabotage/
2. **Paul A. David, "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY" (1985)** — the academic origin: the story entered
   economics literature as fact and became the canonical illustration of path dependence / "lock-in".

**Reported as something everyone believes (the "legend" register), which is how it survives:**
3. **BBC Culture, 13 December 2016** — *"Legend has it that Qwerty was dreamt up with the express purpose
   of slowing typists down."* (the same article then leans toward the jamming explanation).
   https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20161212-why-is-qwerty-on-our-keyboards
4. **BBC News, 23 April 2019** — *"Many people think that qwerty is a bad one – in fact, that it was
   deliberately designed to be slow and awkward."* (this article then explains the telegraph origin).
   https://www.bbc.com/news/business-47460499
5. **The Economic Times, 24 May 2026** — headline: *"The QWERTY keyboard layout **may have been designed
   partly to slow typists down** before typewriters jammed themselves apart"*; in the body it concedes
   "historians disagree about whether QWERTY was designed to slow typists".
   https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/us/the-qwerty-keyboard-layout-may-have-been-designed-partly-to-slow-typists-down-before-typewriters-jammed-themselves-apart/articleshow/131291522.cms

**Honest note on sourcing:** the claim is usually hedged ("legend has it", "many people think", "may have
been") — that hedging is exactly how a 1985 academic assertion turned into common knowledge: every
retelling repeats it as something known, and almost nobody checks the primary record.

## 2. The truth — from the paper that actually examined the primary record

**Yasuoka, K. & Yasuoka, M., "On the Prehistory of QWERTY", ZINBUN 42 (2011), pp. 161–174,
Institute for Research in Humanities, Kyoto University. DOI 10.14989/139379.**
Quotes from the full text:

> "Several papers in the field of information processing answer the question like this: 'To slow down the
> operator.' **It's nonsense.** … The early keyboard of Type-Writer was derived from Hughes-Phelps Printing
> Telegraph, and it was developed for Morse receivers." — Abstract, p. 161

> "Christopher Latham Sholes, the father of 'Type-Writer', did not arrange the typebars in such way.
> **And he never intended to slow down the operator.**" — p. 162

What the letters were actually arranged for (requirement from Harrington & Craig, pp. 163–164):

> "**S should have moved in between Z and E because of the ambiguity of American Morse Code.** The code
> represents Z as '····' which is often confused with the digram SE … Thus S ought to be placed near by
> both Z and E on the keyboard for Morse receivers to type them quickly."

Complementary secondary source: **Smithsonian Magazine** — "some historians have argued that it solved a
jamming problem… others, particularly more recent historians, hold that it was designed specifically to
help telegraph operators." https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/origins-qwerty-keyboard-typewriter-180982726

Neither Sholes' patents (1875–1878) nor contemporary accounts state an intent to reduce typing speed.

## 3. How I investigated

1. Collected the claim as it is stated today (ExplainTheOrdinary, The Economic Times).
2. Traced it to its earliest authoritative statement: not an engineering record, but a 1985 economics paper
   about path dependence.
3. Went to the paper that actually worked through the primary record (patents, telegraph equipment,
   typewritten letters) — and read its full text, not summaries.
4. Cross-checked with a museum-grade secondary account (Smithsonian).
5. Verified the media quotes word for word against the live pages before citing them.

## 4. Why it is interesting

This is a fake fact with a **traceable author**. It did not crystallise from anecdotes: it was introduced by
a highly cited economics paper, became the canonical illustration of "lock-in", and 40 years later is still
presented as engineering history by major outlets — while the people who read the patents say the opposite.
A textbook case of a citation turning into a "fact".
